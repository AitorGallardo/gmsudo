# What it is

I use monkeytype every day, so I rebuilt Typefall into the same thing — a real
typing test — but rendered in three.js instead of on a flat page. By default it
uses a **paragraph** view driven by a measured-flow layout engine: each word is
measured by its actual glyph widths and placed one normal space after the last,
wrapping into a centered, constrained column exactly like prose. Three lines
stay visible so you can read ahead; the active word is bright with a thin green
caret sitting on the next-character boundary, upcoming words are a muted gray,
typed letters turn green and mistakes red. Finish the active line and the whole
block scrolls up one row — the finished line fades out above, a fresh one fades
in below. The column is derived from the camera frustum, so it keeps the same
size and stays centered at 1024, 1440 and 1920, and recomputes on resize without
losing your place. There's also a **stream** view you can switch to, where the
words fly in from the background: the current word big and close, the next few
receding into the dark.

Type it. Every correct keystroke locks that letter into place — it turns green
and holds its slot so the passage never jumps — while a flying copy peels off
and fires the completion effect you picked: it drops and piles on the floor,
bursts into particles, dissolves into ash, spirals off, rockets up with a green
trail, or shatters into shards. Miss a letter and it turns red and the caret
moves on, monkeytype-style. A word finishes the instant you type its last letter
right; if it's still holding a mistake, space is the advance — and any letters
you skipped past are marked as errors, exactly like monkeytype. Backspace walks
back through the current word restoring each letter, and steps into the previous
word too when you left it imperfect, so you can go back and fix it. Finished
words settle into a neutral gray while the word you're on stays bright, and
accuracy counts every keystroke — a corrected mistake still costs you, like it
should.

There's a live WPM and accuracy readout — one quiet mono line, brighter while a
test runs — three modes (timed 15/30/60s, a fixed 10/25/50 words, or endless
zen), and a results screen with WPM, raw, accuracy, character counts and time.
Every config — mode, length and view — keeps its own personal best in
localStorage, so each setup is a separate highscore to chase; beat it and the
screen calls out a new personal best, with your last few runs listed underneath.
Restart is Tab then Enter, same as the muscle memory I already have.

# How it's built

three.js for rendering, cannon-es for physics, plain TypeScript, no framework.
The whole thing is built to hold 60fps on a laptop, which drove most of the
decisions:

- **One cached letter, reused everywhere.** Each character's `TextGeometry` is
  generated once, on demand, and cached. Every word — current, upcoming, the
  ones detaching mid-flight — shares those geometries; only the lightweight
  material and transform differ per instance. Typing the same letter again costs
  nothing.

- **Effects are pools, not allocations.** All the particle effects (explode,
  disintegrate, launch trails, vortex) draw from a single `THREE.Points` cloud
  that's stepped on the CPU and drawn in one call. Shatter shards are a small
  recycled set of boxes integrated by hand. Nothing gets allocated on a
  keystroke during a fast run.

- **Physics only where it earns its keep.** cannon-es runs solely for the *fall*
  effect and the letters that rain behind the results screen, where real
  stacking is the whole point. Live bodies are capped and the oldest fade out,
  so a long test never drifts below frame rate. Shatter fakes its physics
  because it doesn't need contacts — it just needs to look right for a second.

Pixel ratio is capped at 2 and the loop pauses when the tab is hidden. There's a
`window.typefall` handle that can drive a test deterministically, which is how I
verify it.
