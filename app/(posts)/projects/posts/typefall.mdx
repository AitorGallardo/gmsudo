# What it is

I use monkeytype every day, so I rebuilt Typefall into the same thing — a real
typing test — but rendered in three.js instead of on a flat page. By default it
uses a **paragraph** view driven by a measured-flow layout engine. The reading
surface itself is flat, crisp SDF text — a real monospace typeface (JetBrains
Mono) rendered with troika-three-text — so it reads like a proper typing tool
rather than a row of chunky objects. Each word is measured by the font's real
glyph advances and placed one normal space after the last, wrapping into a
centered, constrained column exactly like prose; monospace means every row lines
up perfectly. Three lines stay visible so you can read ahead; the active word is
brighter, upcoming words are a muted gray, typed letters turn green and mistakes
red, finished words settle to a neutral dim. A thin green caret sits on the
next-character boundary and behaves like monkeytype's: it smoothly lerps to the
next slot on every keystroke and blinks at rest, pausing the blink while you
type. Finish the active line and the whole block scrolls up one row — smoothly —
the finished line fading out above, a fresh one fading in below. The column is
derived from the camera frustum, so it keeps the same size and stays centered at
1024, 1440 and 1920, and recomputes on resize without losing your place. Click
away and the passage dims with a small "click to focus" line, just like the real
thing. There's also a **stream** view you can switch to, where the words fly in
from the background as chunky extruded 3D letters: the current word big and
close, the next few receding into the dark.

Type it. Every correct keystroke locks that flat letter into place — it turns
green and holds its slot so the passage never jumps — while a chunky extruded 3D
copy peels off the glyph and fires the completion effect you picked: it drops and
piles on the floor,
bursts into particles, dissolves into ash, spirals off, rockets up with a green
trail, or shatters into shards. Miss a letter and it turns red and the caret
moves on, monkeytype-style. A word finishes the instant you type its last letter
right; if it's still holding a mistake, space is the advance — and any letters
you skipped past are marked as errors, exactly like monkeytype. Backspace walks
back through the current word restoring each letter, and steps into the previous
word too when you left it imperfect, so you can go back and fix it. Finished
words settle into a neutral gray while the word you're on stays bright, and
accuracy counts every keystroke — a corrected mistake still costs you, like it
should.

There's a live WPM and accuracy readout — one quiet mono line, brighter while a
test runs — three modes (timed 15/30/60s, a fixed 10/25/50 words, or endless
zen), and a results screen with WPM, raw, accuracy, character counts and time.
Every config — mode, length and view — keeps its own personal best in
localStorage, so each setup is a separate highscore to chase; beat it and the
screen calls out a new personal best, with your last few runs listed underneath.
Restart is Tab then Enter, same as the muscle memory I already have.

# How it's built

three.js for rendering, troika-three-text for the flat reading surface,
cannon-es for physics, plain TypeScript, no framework. The whole thing is built
to hold 60fps on a laptop, which drove most of the decisions:

- **The right tool for each surface.** The paragraph you read is SDF text
  (troika-three-text), one glyph node per character: crisp at any size, real
  font metrics, and cheap — three full lines plus a dozen live clones measure
  around 2ms a frame. Color changes are just a material uniform, so a keystroke
  only recolors a glyph and nudges the caret — never a geometry rebuild. The
  chunky 3D letters — the stream view and every clone that detaches and falls —
  use extruded `TextGeometry`, generated once per character and cached, so those
  bevelled glyphs are shared by every instance and typing the same letter again
  costs nothing.

- **Effects are pools, not allocations.** All the particle effects (explode,
  disintegrate, launch trails, vortex) draw from a single `THREE.Points` cloud
  that's stepped on the CPU and drawn in one call. Shatter shards are a small
  recycled set of boxes integrated by hand. Nothing gets allocated on a
  keystroke during a fast run.

- **Physics only where it earns its keep.** cannon-es runs solely for the *fall*
  effect and the letters that rain behind the results screen, where real
  stacking is the whole point. Live bodies are capped and the oldest fade out,
  so a long test never drifts below frame rate. Shatter fakes its physics
  because it doesn't need contacts — it just needs to look right for a second.

Pixel ratio is capped at 2 and the loop pauses when the tab is hidden. There's a
`window.typefall` handle that can drive a test deterministically, which is how I
verify it.
